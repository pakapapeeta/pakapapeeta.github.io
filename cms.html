§1 Motivation

Why learn detector simulation and reconstruction at all?

The LHC's high-luminosity era needs orders of magnitude more simulated events than the collaborations can afford with Geant4 full simulation, where a single event can take minutes. The usual escape hatch is Delphes — a fast, parametric detector model — but its hand-tuned smearing and efficiency functions cap how faithfully it can reproduce a real experiment.

The Parnassus idea is to replace that chain with a single learned surrogate: a generative model trained on full simulation that maps generator-level (truth) particles directly to reconstructed particle-flow objects — collapsing detector response and event reconstruction into one GPU-native step, and generalising beyond any parametric card because it learns the true response from data.

This repo is the event-level member of that programme:

◆ The one-sentence thesis

Two conditional flow-matching networks — one for event-level quantities, one for per-particle objects — are trained to sample the distribution of reconstructed events given the truth-level input, and are chained at inference to fast-simulate whole events.

§2 Data

How an event is represented

Everything lives in utils/datasetloader.py (FastSimDataset), reading ROOT ntuples with uproot. Each event is two variable-length, unordered sets of particles — truth (the conditioning input) and pflow (the reconstruction target) — padded to max_particles = 400 with boolean masks.

The five classes are charged hadrons, electrons, muons, neutral hadrons, photons (neutrinos and residuals are handled in pre-processing). Alongside the per-particle sets, each event carries global quantities — scalar Hₜ, missing-transverse-energy components (MET_x, MET_y), and multiplicity N — computed for both truth and pflow.

◆ The clever bit — per-event scaling (PES)

For each event, the truth kinematics define their own mean/std, and that same transform is applied to the pflow targets (_get_pflow_data reuses truth_vars_scales). The network therefore learns the truth→reco relationship in a per-event normalised frame, and the shift/scale values are handed back as conditioning ("scale info"). This is a strong inductive bias worth being able to explain.

§3 Architecture

Two specialised networks, trained separately

The decomposition is deliberate: event-level totals are global and low-dimensional, so a small network fixes how many particles and how much energy first; the heavy set-generation network then produces what each particle is, conditioned on those totals.

Stage 1 Event network — FlowNumPFNet

In models/flow_npf_model.py. Truth particles are embedded and mean-pooled (permutation-invariant); concatenated with the embedded global data and a sinusoidal time embedding to form a context vector. A 4-dimensional noisy vector (Hₜ, N, MET_x, MET_y) is modulated by that context (adaLN), passed through residual blocks, and mapped to a velocity. Small and quick: hidden_dim 128, 3 residual blocks, GELU.

Stage 2 Particle network — FlowNet (CA-DiT)

In models/flow_model.py — the core of the paper. It runs two token streams: the truth particles and the noisy pflow particles. Each CABlock (there are num_dit_layers = 8) performs bidirectional cross-attention — the pflow stream attends to truth, and truth attends back to pflow (all but the final block) — with the context vector driving a DiT-style adaLN-Zero modulation (six shift/scale/gate terms per block).

  • Cross-attention via models/attention.py — PyTorch FlexAttention with per-head QK-norm, padding masks so padded tokens never send information.
  • adaLN-Zero: modulation layers are zero-initialised (initialize_weights), so every block starts as the identity — the DiT stability trick.
  • RMSNorm + gated-GLU MLPs; hidden_dim 96, 6 heads, SiLU.
  • Context = concat[ pooled-truth summary, global embedding, time embedding ] — dimension 3 × hidden.

Event net facts

  • output: 4 scalars Hₜ, N, MET_x, MET_y
  • flow variant: TargetCFM
  • sampler: PNDM · 25 steps
  • power-law time sampling (time_pow)

Particle net facts

  • output: 7 features per particle × up to 400
  • flow variant: ReverseCFM
  • sampler: DPM-Solver · 10–40 steps
  • bidirectional CA-DiT, 8 layers

§4 The method

Flow matching, and the bridge to diffusion

Both networks are trained by conditional flow matching: rather than denoising, the network learns a velocity field v(x, t) that transports Gaussian noise x₀ to data x₁ along a straight probability path. Training is simulation-free — you sample a time, form the interpolant, and regress onto a closed-form target velocity. No adversary, no ELBO.

The two stages use different straight paths — utils/conditional_flow_matching.py defines ReverseCFM (particles: u_t = x₀ − x₁) and TargetCFM (events, Lipman-style optimal-transport path). Stage 2 adds a cosine direction term to the MSE so predicted and target velocities also agree in orientation.

◆ Why a diffusion solver samples a flow-matching model

The sampler in models/dpm.py uses NoiseScheduleFlow with α_t = 1 − t, σ_t = t — the linear / rectified-flow schedule. Under it the flow-matching ODE and the diffusion probability-flow ODE coincide, so the learned velocity can be fed to a DPM-Solver after a one-line wrapper converts velocity to the data-prediction form the solver expects. That is the whole "flows ↔ diffusion" trick, and it is why the repo can use a high-order diffusion integrator on a flow-matching network.

§5 Training

Loss, optimiser, and a subtle train/val split

train.py builds a PyTorch-Lightning module chosen by train_type (evt → event net, else particle net) and calls trainer.fit. Optimiser is Lion at 3e-5 with cosine-annealing warm restarts, gradient clipping, and Stochastic Weight Averaging; logging via Comet.

▲ A distinction interviewers may probe

The training loss is velocity regression applied position-wise — flow matching pairs each noised sample with its own target by index, so no set-matching is needed at train time. The permutation-invariant Set2SetLoss (Hungarian assignment via scipy.linear_sum_assignment) is a validation-only metric: it runs the full ODE sampler, then matches generated to true particles on pt/eta/phi. Don't call it the training objective.

Train signal

  • Stage 1: per-component MSE on the 4 event scalars
  • Stage 2: MSE + (1 − cosine), masked to real particles
  • targets are analytic flow-matching velocities

Validation signal

  • generate with the ODE sampler, then compare
  • Stage 2: Hungarian-matched Set2Set on kinematics
  • Stage 1: MSE on Hₜ / N / MET

§6 Inference

Chaining the two stages at generation time

eval.py is where the stages meet — and the linkage lives here, not inside either network:

  • Stage 1 (FlowNumPFLightning.sample, PNDM — a 4th-order pseudo-numerical integrator in models/sampler.py) samples N, Hₜ, MET for each event.
  • Those predictions build the particle mask (setting the generated multiplicity) and are concatenated into the Stage-2 global conditioning.
  • Stage 2 (DPM-Solver, multistep order-2, time_uniform_flow schedule) integrates the velocity field from noise to the reconstructed PF objects.
  • Outputs are inverse-transformed (class via argmax, φ back from sin/cos) and written to a ROOT evt_tree with truth and pflow branches.

◆ The mental model

Stage 1 answers "how many particles and how much energy"; Stage 2 answers "what is each particle", conditioned on Stage 1's answer plus the truth particles. Integrating a learned ODE from Gaussian noise turns both questions into samples from the correct conditional distribution.

Q1 Why generative models instead of a regression network?

Detector response and reconstruction are stochastic and one-to-many: a given truth configuration maps to a distribution of possible reconstructions (resolution smearing, efficiency losses, track–cluster splitting and merging). A generative model samples p(reco | truth), preserving resolutions and correlations. A plain regressor would collapse to the conditional mean and wash those out.

Q2 Why flow matching rather than diffusion, GANs, or VAEs?

Flow matching trains by regressing a closed-form velocity — stable, mode-covering, no adversarial collapse and no ELBO slack. Straight probability paths make sampling cheap (few function evaluations). And because the model is just a velocity field, it slots straight into ODE solvers — here they reuse a diffusion DPM-Solver through a rectified-flow schedule (α=1−t, σ=t), getting high-order integration for free.

Q3 Why split it into two networks?

The event-level quantities (multiplicity, Hₜ, MET) are global and low-dimensional. Generating them first fixes the set cardinality and provides strong global conditioning, decoupling "how many / how much" from "what each particle is." It also lets each network be small and specialised, and keeps the hard set-generation problem cleanly conditioned.

Q4 How do you handle variable-length, unordered particle sets?

Pad to 400 with masks; the particle network is permutation-equivariant (attention, no positional encoding). Training loss is position-wise because flow matching pairs noise↔target by index. The evaluation metric is permutation-invariant — a Hungarian assignment (Set2Set) between generated and true particles.

Q5 What exactly conditions the particle network?

Four things, fused through DiT adaLN: (1) the truth particles as cross-attention keys/values, (2) a permutation-invariant pooled truth summary, (3) the Stage-1 event-level N, Hₜ, MET plus per-event scale info in the global vector, and (4) the diffusion time embedding.

Q6 How are particle type and production vertex produced?

Jointly with the kinematics. Class is a 5-way one-hot generated as continuous logits and argmax-ed at sampling; the vertex (vx,vy,vz) are continuous outputs (neutral particles have their vertex zeroed in pre-processing). Generating type and vertex is exactly what extends Parnassus from jets to full PF objects.

Q7 What would you improve or study next? (shows depth)

Concrete, defensible directions:

• Sampling efficiency — the step counts and integrators are fixed heuristics; since the model is a velocity field, one can swap in adaptive solvers or distil to very few steps with no retraining, and measure the Wasserstein/observable cost vs. NFE.

• Two-stage error propagation — Stage-1 multiplicity/energy mistakes bias Stage 2; worth quantifying and possibly jointly fine-tuning.

• Physics closure & calibration — coverage of tails and inter-particle correlations, jet-level and MET observables, and per-event uncertainty.

• Conditioning & scope — pileup, richer event context, and generalisation across processes beyond the training set.

Q8 Where does this sit relative to Delphes and full sim?

It is trained on full-simulation CMS Open Sim, so it learns the true detector + reconstruction response rather than a parametric approximation — the paper shows it generalising beyond Delphes across several LHC processes, while remaining fast and GPU-native. The downstream parnassus package then wraps such trained models into an end-to-end tool.
